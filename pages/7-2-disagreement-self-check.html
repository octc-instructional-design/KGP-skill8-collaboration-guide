Section 7.2

How I Handle Disagreement

A short Week 1 self-check — six questions, no score, no result. You'll come back to it at the end of the project.

Complete in Week 1  ·  Private to you  ·  Revisit at the end

This isn't a personality test and there's no result at the end. Nobody is going to tell you that you're “an avoider” or “a competitor.” Most people handle disagreement differently depending on who they're with, how tired they are, and how much they care about the outcome.

What it's for: writing down where you're starting, so that at the end of this project you can look back and see what actually changed. That's hard to notice from memory — people tend to remember themselves as having always been the way they are now.

Six Questions

  1. Think of a time a group you were in disagreed about something. What did you actually do?
    A few sentences. Any group counts — work, family, a class, a team.
  2. When a disagreement starts in a group, my first instinct is usually to…
    • say what I think right away
    • wait and see what other people say first
    • look for a middle ground quickly so it doesn't escalate
    • stay quiet and let it pass
    • something else — describe it
    None of these is the right answer. Pick the one that's most often true for you, and add a note if it depends.

  4. One thing I find hard about it:
    Everyone has one. Naming it now is the point of the exercise.
  5. What I'd want teammates to know about how I come across when I'm frustrated, rushed, or overloaded:
    Especially in writing, where there's no tone of voice to go on.
  6. One thing I want to get better at during this project:

Keep this. At the end of the project you'll be asked to look back at what you wrote here — especially questions 4 and 6 — and say what's different now.

For Faculty

  • This is a baseline, not a grouping tool. Form teams from the Collaboration Profile Survey — availability and strengths are far more reliable than a first-week self-report about conflict. Don't sort teams by what students write here.
  • It's your best evidence for 8.4 at Benchmark. The CPE Benchmark for 8.4 is “acknowledges both strengths and limitations within a team” — that's questions 3 and 4, in the student's own words, before the project starts. Nothing else in the guide captures that at entry.
  • Grade for completion, or not at all. Scoring honesty about your own limitations teaches students to write what looks good instead of what's true.
  • Don't type students, and don't let the instrument do it either. Question 2 has options because a blank page is hard to start on, not because the answers mean something. If you find yourself thinking of a student as “the avoider,” the instrument has done harm.
  • Close the loop, or skip the whole thing. A self-check collected and never mentioned again is busywork. Its entire value is the comparison at the end — the exemplar reflection prompts are written to ask for it.
  • Keep it separate from the survey. The survey is yours, for forming teams. This one is the student's, and they need it back later. Different owner, different purpose, different visibility.

Also in Week 1: the Collaboration Profile Survey in 7.0 — that's the one you use to form teams.  The indicator this feeds: Indicator 8.4 · Adaptability & Trust.  Where it's used: Week 1 of both Sustained exemplars in 9.0.
