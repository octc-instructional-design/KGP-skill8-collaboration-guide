Section 4.4

Running a Sustained Team Project

The decisions to make before you build a multi-week project — true for any discipline.

Level 4 · Sustained  ·  Decide These Six Things First

What this page is — and isn't. 4.0 tells you when to run a sustained project: only when you need 8.4. The exemplars in 9.0 show fully worked projects. This page is the layer in between — the decisions every sustained project has to make, so you can design your own instead of adapting someone else's.

These are design decisions, made before students arrive. What you do once it's running — how much to watch, when to step in, how to close it out — is 2.0 | Facilitating Effective Group Work.

Why a sustained project needs planning the lighter levels don't: length is the whole difference. Weeks mean attrition, changing conditions, and room for a passenger to hide — none of which a one-class activity has to survive. Each decision below, left unmade, is where these projects quietly fail.

The Six Decisions

  1. Part 1: Why this needs a team at all

    Decide What makes this task impossible for one student to do alone?

    Best practice Answer this before anything else, because every other decision depends on it. The test: could one motivated student do this well over a long weekend? If yes, it isn't a team task — it's parallel work with a shared grade, and that is precisely what produces the resentment faculty remember from their own group projects. Genuine interdependence usually comes from one of four places: divided evidence or expertise (each member holds something the others need), sequence (one person's output is the next person's input), built-in conflict (the sources genuinely disagree and the team must reconcile them), or scale (the work is simply larger than one person's term). The worked exemplars in 9.0 each name their engine explicitly — that's the part worth borrowing.

  2. Part 2: Group size & formation

    Decide How big should teams be, and who forms them?

    Best practice Start teams at five, with four as the floor, faculty-assigned using the Collaboration Profile Survey in 7.0. With real attrition, design for the team you'll have in week six, not week one: a group of five that loses one or two still works with its roles intact, while a group of four that loses two collapses into a pair. The cost of five is a higher chance of a passenger — which is exactly what roles and accountability in Parts 4 and 6 exist to catch. Treat size and accountability as one decision, not two. (For short Coordinated tasks, three or four is plenty — attrition only bites over weeks.)

  3. Part 3: Where the work lives

    Decide Where do students collaborate, and where does graded work go?

    Best practice One boundary, told to students on day one: Teams is the workspace (chat, planning, co-editing files); Blackboard is the official record (submissions, rubric, grades). Nothing graded lives only in Teams. Most “lost work” problems are really “submitted in the wrong place” problems. Settings that matter for both are in 8.0 | Technology Tools.

  4. Part 4: Roles

    Decide Who is responsible for what?

    Best practice Assign roles by strength, using the survey, not by who volunteers first. Four core roles — team coordinator, researcher, writer/editor, designer — cover most projects; in a group of five, the extra member adds slack or pairs with the role that needs depth. Let roles rotate at the midpoint if the team wants. This is where Indicator 8.3 lives, and it should follow directly from Part 1: if the interdependence engine is divided evidence, the roles are the divisions.

  5. Part 5: Scaffolding & cadence

    Decide One big deadline, or checkpoints along the way?

    Best practice Never one big due date. Break the project into milestones — survey, contract, plan, draft, midpoint check, final, reflection — so a struggling team surfaces early enough to save. A weekly light-touch check-in beats a single late rescue. Each milestone is also an assessment point, which means you are never grading a semester of teamwork from one artifact at the end.

  6. Part 6: Accountability & assessment

    Decide How does individual accountability ride alongside a shared grade?

    Best practice Use the three-part model in 5.0 — Product (shared), Process (individual, from peer evaluation), Reflection (individual). The two highest-leverage pieces are a signed group contract up front and a confidential midpoint peer evaluation; both templates are in 7.0. Build the grade from the indicators using 6.0 | Build Your Rubric. This apparatus is what makes a group of five safe — without it, size just hides the passenger.

Once it's running, the job changes from designing to watching — roughly fifteen minutes a week for a project this size. What to watch for, when to intervene and when deliberately not to, and how to close it out are all in 2.0 | Facilitating Effective Group Work. Design well here and there is far less to do there.

For Faculty

  • Part 1 is the one that decides whether this works. Every complaint about group projects — free-riding, uneven effort, “I could have done it faster myself” — traces back to a task that didn't actually require a team.
  • Size and accountability are one decision. A group of five only works if roles, individual deliverables, and peer evaluation are in place to catch a passenger.
  • Milestones beat late rescues. Most group-work disasters are late-detection problems, not bad-student problems.
  • Pilot one project before redesigning a whole course around team work. Reuse the contract and survey every term once they're set.
  • Say the Teams/Blackboard boundary out loud on day one. It prevents the most common — and most frustrating — submission problem.
