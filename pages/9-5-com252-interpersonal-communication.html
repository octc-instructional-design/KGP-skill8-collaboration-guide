Section 9.5

Interpersonal Communication in Action

A fully online COM 252 project where a team builds a shared resource together — and then turns the lens on itself, analyzing its own communication with the concepts the course teaches.

Level 4 · Sustained  ·  Online COM 252  ·  Surfaces all four indicators

Alignment. Targets the Benchmark → Milestone progression over a fuller term — about eight to ten weeks, four- or five-person teams, working in Blackboard Groups and a Microsoft Teams channel. The design does double duty: the team produces a content deliverable and uses its own collaboration as the case it analyzes, which serves the COM 252 outcomes on listening, perception, conflict strategies, communication climate, and culture all at once. The friction of online teamwork is not a problem to design around here — it's the authentic material the course exists to teach.

Why This Needs a Team — and Works Online

The resource itself runs on divided expertise — research, writing, and design are genuinely different contributions, which is the first engine in Level 4 · Sustained. But the more interesting thing this project does is with accountability.

COM 252 teaches listening, perception, communication climate, conflict, and culture — and an online team is a live setting for every one of them. When a teammate misreads a blunt message, when a channel goes silent for three days, when two members default to different conflict styles, that isn't a logistics headache; it's the exact phenomenon the course teaches students to recognize. So the team's shared process becomes each student's individual assessment data. Four people live through the same weeks and write four different analyses, because they noticed different things and played different parts.

That solves the hardest problem in group grading without any additional apparatus: individual accountability isn't bolted on, it's what the course was assessing anyway. And it makes the analysis authentic rather than hypothetical — the data is the students' own experience.

The Scenario

Your team is creating a practical resource that helps people communicate better in a context you choose — managing conflict with a roommate, listening across cultural differences, building a positive climate at work, navigating self-disclosure online. Over the next several weeks you'll research and build that resource together, entirely online. But there's a second layer: while you collaborate, your team is itself an example of interpersonal communication. You'll watch how your team listens, handles disagreement, and forms a climate — and each of you will write an analysis applying course concepts to your team's real experience.

Faculty note: let teams choose the resource topic from a short menu so they have ownership; the self-analysis layer is what carries the Skill 8 and course-concept work, so it stays required.

Roles

Assign by the strengths surfaced in the Week 1 survey. The fifth role is the course-specific one and is worth including when team size allows.

  • Team Coordinator — schedules, tracks progress, submits to Blackboard, and models the team's communication climate.
  • Lead Researcher — gathers the concepts and evidence the resource is built on.
  • Writer / Editor — pulls the parts into one clear voice.
  • Designer / Producer — builds the resource itself (document, slides, or a simple shared site).
  • Process Observer / Climate Keeper (five-person teams) — keeps the shared log of how the team is communicating, giving everyone richer material for the final analysis.

Online logistics: the team's Teams channel is both the workspace and the object of study — chat, files, and meetings all live there, and they become evidence for the final analysis. The Blackboard Group set holds the submitted resource and the rubric; build the group set before the assignment, per 8.0 | Technology Tools.

A facilitation note peculiar to this course: intervene less than you otherwise would. A team that works through its own rough patch has written its own analysis material; a team you rescue has less to write about. The exception is the usual one — step in when someone goes quiet, not when people disagree. See 2.0 | Facilitating Effective Group Work.

Assessment — The Three-Part Model

The reflection part is heavier than usual here: the Communication Analysis is the reflection, expanded, because applying course concepts to real interaction is the central skill of COM 252. The two individual parts come to 50% of the grade — comfortably above the one-third floor described in 5.0, which is why the shared grade can't hide a passenger.

Student Instructions (copy into the assignment)

  1. Set up your team space and climate. In your Teams channel, complete the Group Contract and agree on communication norms — how you'll respond, how you'll handle disagreement, how often you'll check in.
  2. Choose your topic. Pick the interpersonal-communication context your resource will help with.
  3. Research and build together. Each role contributes; the resource comes together in your shared Teams files.
  4. Watch your own team. As you work, notice real moments — a misread message, a good repair, a silence, a strong listening move. Keep notes; these are your analysis data.
  5. Workshop and revise. Exchange drafts with another team, give specific feedback, and revise yours on what you receive.
  6. Finalize and submit. Your Team Coordinator submits the resource in Blackboard. Teams is where you work; Blackboard is where it counts.
  7. Analyze and reflect. Write your individual Communication Analysis, then complete the final peer evaluation.

Communication Analysis Prompt (individual)

Apply at least three course concepts — for example listening, perception, communication climate, conflict style, self-disclosure, or nonverbal cues in text — to real moments in your team's communication. For each: describe what actually happened, explain it with the concept, and say what more competent communication would have looked like. Then reread your Week 1 Disagreement Self-Check and use it as your fourth piece of evidence: what did you predict about yourself, what actually happened, and what accounts for the difference? Close by naming where you grew in “collaborate, be an effective team member, and manage conflict,” and where you're still developing.

Tell students up front that a team with friction is not a team with a worse analysis — usually the opposite. Otherwise the honest ones worry that describing a rough patch will cost them.

Adapting This for Your Course

  • The self-analysis layer is the durable move. Any course that teaches communication, teamwork, leadership, or organizational behavior can have the team analyze its own process — swap in that course's concepts.
  • It's also the cleanest individual accountability there is. Four students share one experience and write four different analyses. Nobody can free-ride through a paper about what they personally did.
  • Swap the resource topic freely. The deliverable is the occasion for collaboration; the learning is in doing it and examining it.
  • Build your own resource rubric from the indicators using 6.0 | Build Your Rubric; the process rubric above transfers unchanged.
