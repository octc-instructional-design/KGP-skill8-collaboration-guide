Section 9.2

Structured Collaboration: Idea Bank

Short exchanges with a little structure that build Indicator 8.2 — communicating clearly, giving and receiving feedback, handling disagreement. Sorted by field, meant to be borrowed.

Level 2 · Structured  ·  Builds 8.2  ·  Borrow Freely

This is a brainstorm, not a worked plan. Every idea here is Structured: a short activity with a protocol where students respond to and build on each other's work — a class or a few days, builds Indicator 8.2. The difference from Quick is the exchange: there's always a response, not just a post. The feedback a student writes is the evidence.

To run any of these online: the exchange lives in a Blackboard Discussion (post, then reply to assigned peers) or Blackboard's peer review for asynchronous — in a Teams meeting for live. Set two deadlines, not one: post by an earlier date, respond by a later one. A single deadline produces a pile-up with nothing to respond to.

Works in Any Course

  • Two stars and a step. Each student reads a classmate's short piece and names two specific strengths and one concrete next step.
  • Sentence-stem reply. Students must respond to a peer using a required opener — “Building on…,” “I see it differently because…” — so disagreement stays professional.
  • Respond and extend. Replies can't be “I agree” — each must add evidence, an example, or a counterpoint to the peer's idea.
  • Critique the model. The class gives structured feedback on an anonymous or instructor-provided sample before critiquing each other — safe practice in specific, respectful notes.
  • Revise and report. After getting feedback, each student posts one line on what they changed and why — the clearest evidence of adjusting based on feedback.
  • Steelman it. Before arguing your position, state the strongest version of the opposing view so a peer would recognize it as fair.

By Field

These are browsing aids, not boundaries. Steal from any cluster — a plan review works just as well on a care plan, a lesson plan, or a business proposal.

Health Sciences & Allied Health

  • Hand-off feedback. A classmate posts a patient hand-off; peers give one note on clarity or completeness using a short checklist.
  • SBAR review. Each reviews a partner's Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation note and suggests one specific improvement.
  • Reasonable disagreement. Given a scenario where clinicians could differ, take a position and respond professionally to a peer who chose otherwise.
  • Patient-language critique. Review a sample patient explanation; each suggests one way to make it clearer or more compassionate.

Technical & Trades

  • Plan review. A classmate posts a work or safety plan; peers each give one specific, actionable improvement.
  • Defend your method. Two valid approaches to a task — each argues for one and responds to a peer's choice without dismissing it.
  • Spec-check feedback. A peer reviews a described build, weld, or wiring against the spec and names one thing to correct.
  • Near-miss build. Respond to a posted near-miss with one corrective action that builds on what classmates already suggested.

Business, IT & Office

  • Email redline. A peer reviews a draft professional email and gives two specific edits for tone and clarity.
  • Pitch feedback. A classmate posts a two-line pitch; peers each respond with one strength and one sharpening question.
  • Constructive code review. A peer checks a short snippet or formula and leaves one comment — what works, and one thing to fix.
  • Strengthen the reply. Given a customer's pushback, each drafts a professional response, then peers suggest one way to make it stronger.

General Education & First-Year

  • Thesis feedback. A peer reads a classmate's thesis statement and offers one specific way to sharpen it.
  • Respond with evidence. Reply to a peer's claim by adding a source that supports or complicates it — not just an opinion.
  • Structured debate. Assigned positions; each must restate the other side fairly before countering it.
  • Explain and check. Post your solution method or reasoning; a peer responds with where they followed it and where they lost the thread — feedback on communication, not just the answer.

Making One Yours

  • Grade the feedback, not the original. In a Structured move the skill is the quality of the exchange — a short rubric for “specific, respectful, actionable” assesses 8.2 directly.
  • Two deadlines, not one. Post by one date, respond by a later one. It's the single change that makes an async exchange actually an exchange.
  • Give them stems or criteria. Openers for agreeing, extending, and respectfully disagreeing keep online exchanges professional and on topic — and they matter more in writing, where there's no tone and no quick repair.
  • Practice on a model first. Critique-the-model lowers the stakes before students review each other's real work.
  • Assign who reviews whom. Left open, the same students get reviewed twice and someone gets nothing back.
  • Adult and dual-credit learners: the async version — a Discussion exchange over a couple of days — lets students respond around work and caregiving.
