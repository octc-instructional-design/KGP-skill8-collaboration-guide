Design Your Assessment

Backward design from outcome to task — the step between choosing your level and building your rubric.

Why this page exists: a rubric scores a task — so before you can build one, you need a task that's actually defined. This page walks the decisions that turn “I picked a level” into “I have an assessment ready to score.” It follows backward design: start from the outcome, end with the evidence, and only then build the rubric.

It works at any level. Answer the decisions that fit your size and move on — a Quick activity needs only the first few; a Sustained project needs all six. The point isn't a long form; it's making sure no decision gets skipped that you'll regret later.

Six Decisions, In Order

  1. Decision 1: Start from the outcome

    Decide Which indicators are you building, and at what level?

    Backward design starts here, not with an activity. Name the indicator (8.1–8.4) and the level it calls for. If you haven't sized it yet, do that first — the level shapes every decision below.

    Not sure of the level? 4.0 | Choosing Your Approach.

  2. Decision 2: Confirm it should be collaborative

    Decide Does this genuinely need a team — or is it faster and fairer done alone?

    Group work earns its place only when the task truly needs more than one person. The test: could one motivated student do this well on their own in the time available? If yes, make it individual. A task that doesn't require a team but carries a shared grade is exactly what produces the resentment students bring to the next one. This gate is also how you justify the assignment to students who dread group work — you can tell them why it takes a team.

    At project scale, the four sources of real interdependence are in Level 4 · Sustained.

  3. Decision 3: Define the task and the product

    Decide What will students actually do, and what will they hand in?

    Write the task in a sentence or two and name the deliverable. Keep it as small as the outcome allows — the task should match the level you chose, not exceed it. Over-building is the most common design error on this page.

    Need concrete, run-as-is examples at your level? The four level pages, 4.1 through 4.4, and 9.0 | Examples.

  4. Decision 4: Decide what's individual and what's shared

    Decide What's graded together, and what's graded alone?

    Every collaborative assessment needs a visible individual thread, or a single shared grade hides who did what. It's the decision faculty skip and regret. At Coordinated and Sustained, the reliable structure is three parts:

    Product

    The deliverable the team produces together.

    Shared grade

    Process

    How the student worked with the team — from peer evaluation and the contribution record.

    Individual grade

    Reflection

    The student's own account of what they contributed and what they'd change.

    Individual grade

    A workable starting split for a Sustained project is roughly 50–60% Product, 20–30% Process, 15–20% Reflection — adjust to your course, but keep the two individual parts together above about a third, or the shared grade dominates and the passenger problem returns. At Coordinated, a lighter version works: the shared deliverable plus one individual piece. At Quick and Structured there's no group grade at all, so this decision mostly disappears.

  5. Decision 5: Design for access from the start

    Decide Can every student take part — on their device, in their schedule, with their needs?

    Build access in; don't bolt it on. Offer flexible roles and more than one way to contribute; caption media and check contrast; give asynchronous windows so working, caregiving, and dual-credit students can take part on their own time. Keeping the work in Blackboard and Teams also means there's no new account to create and nothing to install, which matters most for students on school-issued Chromebooks. (This is Universal Design for Learning: multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression.)

  6. Decision 6: Name your evidence

    Decide What exactly will you look at to judge the outcome?

    Point to the specific artifact — the discussion thread, the shared deliverable, the peer evaluation, the reflection. That artifact is what your rubric will score, and the observable behaviors visible in it become your criteria. If you can't name the evidence, the task isn't defined yet.

    Ready to turn evidence into criteria and levels? 6.0 | Build Your Rubric.

Scale this to your level. For a Quick or Structured activity, settle the first few decisions in minutes and go straight to participation or the rubric. For Coordinated and Sustained, work through all six — and for a Sustained project, Level 4 carries the planning the rest of the way.

For Faculty

  • Backward design, every time: outcome → task → evidence → rubric. The rubric is the last step, not the first.
  • Decision 4 is the one to make before the project starts, not after a complaint. Individual accountability designed in is worth more than any intervention later — and it's what makes the intervention ladder in 2.0 workable when you do need it.
  • Right-size the task to the outcome. If you're designing something bigger than the indicator needs, you've over-scaled — step back to 4.0.
  • Access designed in costs less than accommodations bolted on, and it serves every student, not only the one who asked.
