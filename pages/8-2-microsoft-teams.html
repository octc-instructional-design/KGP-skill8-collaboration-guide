8.2 Using Microsoft Teams for Group Work

Not a how-to-click-Teams guide — the group-work decisions and boundaries for using Teams as a team's workspace.

What to Use, What to Settle First, What Bites

This page assumes you can use Teams at a basic level and know where to get help — so it skips the click-by-click steps (which change, and differ between the desktop and web apps) and covers only what matters for group work: which Teams feature fits which job, the few choices worth getting right before students start, and the one boundary that prevents most problems. Current steps live at the support links below.

Settle These Before Students Start

  • A channel per team, not a Team per project. One class Team with a channel for each group is far simpler to manage than spinning up separate Teams. Reserve a full Team for large, long-running efforts.
  • Sort out access first, and test it. Confirm that every member — and you — can actually get in before any work depends on it. Guest and dual-credit accounts are the most common blocker, and they fail quietly: the student sees nothing rather than an error you'd hear about.
  • Name the single home for the work. Point students to the Files tab and say that's where the current version lives. Naming one home prevents most of the “which copy is the real one” chaos.
  • Recording. Know where recordings save and who can see them, and tell students when a session is being recorded.
  • Set notification expectations. Tell teams how often you expect them to check in. Without that, students either drown in alerts or miss everything — and the group contract's response window is the place to write it down.

Boundaries & Gotchas

Teams is the workspace. Blackboard is the official record. Planning, chat, and drafting happen here; the graded deliverable submits in Blackboard. Say so on day one.

  • Don't grade from Teams. When the work is done, the team brings the finished file back to the Blackboard assignment.
  • You can see the channels — say so, and don't manage the chatter. Tell students in week one that you can see channel activity; monitoring that's announced changes behavior usefully, monitoring that's discovered later costs you trust. Watch milestones and silences, not the daily back-and-forth.
  • Teams doesn't tell you who did what. A co-authored file shows a final product, not contribution. Individual accountability has to come from the assignment design, not from the tool.
