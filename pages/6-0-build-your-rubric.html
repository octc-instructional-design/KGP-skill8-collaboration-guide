Build Your Rubric

Turn the four indicators into a rubric that fits your own assignment.

From Observable Behaviors to a Rubric You Own

You are here · 6.0Build Your Rubric

How to think about this page. It is not a rubric to adopt as-is, and it is not a checklist students get scored against. It is a bank of observable behaviors for Skill 8 — the same ones on the four indicator cards. You pick the ones your assignment actually calls for, then write performance levels in your own words for your own task. The result is a rubric you own — specific, gradable, and defensible — rather than a generic teamwork score.

Four Steps

  • Name what the assignment exercises. Look at your group task and decide which one to three indicators it genuinely involves. A short discussion task may be only 8.1; a multi-week project with a mid-course change may be all four. Don't force-fit indicators that aren't really there.
  • Lift the behaviors. From the bank below, copy the specific behaviors under your chosen indicators. These become your rubric criteria — one row each.
  • Write your own levels. For each behavior, describe what it looks like at each level of your scale, in the language of your assignment. Use any scale you already use; two starter scales are below.
  • Weight and place it. Decide how much each criterion is worth and how the collaboration score relates to the product grade. Keeping process and product visible as separate things is usually fairer than one blended number — that's the three-part model in 5.0.

The Behavior Bank

All four indicators in one place. The milestone target names the two-year goal; the checked items are the observable behaviors you can lift into a rubric.

Indicator 8.1
Contributing to Team Goals
Milestone target:  Contributes ideas aligned to identified objectives of the team.Assessable at every level, 1 Quick through 4 Sustained
  • Offers at least one idea or suggestion during team discussions that directly relates to the team's stated goal
  • Explains how their contribution connects to the team's objectives when asked
  • Weighs in when the team makes a decision rather than going along with whatever is proposed first
  • Builds on others' ideas rather than redirecting the conversation away from team goals
Indicator 8.2
Professional Communication & Feedback
Milestone target:  Addresses conflicts constructively, communicating expectations and adjusting own behavior based on feedback received.Assessable from level 2 · Structured onward (partly at level 1)
  • Responds to team conflict or disagreement without dismissing it, avoiding it, or escalating it
  • States a concern or expectation directly to the team rather than working around it or bringing it only to the instructor
  • Changes their approach after receiving peer or instructor feedback — and can say what they changed
  • Gives feedback that names something specific and suggests a next step, rather than a general reaction
Indicator 8.3
Roles & Responsibilities
Milestone target:  Identifies tasks required to reach a goal and learns about team members' assets to support the distribution of roles and responsibilities.Assessable from level 3 · Coordinated onward
  • Participates in breaking a team goal into specific, assignable tasks
  • Identifies at least one strength or asset each teammate brings to the project
  • Accepts and fulfills a role assigned on the basis of team need, not just personal preference
  • Delivers their assigned piece by the date the team agreed on — or tells the team before it's late
Indicator 8.4
Adaptability & Trust
Milestone target:  Adapts to changing project requirements and roles in a way that fosters mutual trust and respect amongst team members.Assessable at level 4 · Sustained (partly at level 3) — things need time to change
  • Adjusts their approach or role when project requirements shift, rather than continuing with the original plan
  • Tells the team when their own circumstances will affect the work — before it affects the work
  • Takes on a task outside their original role when the team needs it covered
  • Keeps working with teammates after a disagreement rather than withdrawing or going around them

Two starter scales — use whichever fits, or your own:

Developmental (3 levels): Not yet · Approaching · Meets the goal. Good for first-year and entry courses, where many students are honestly still moving from benchmark toward milestone — a low rating here means “still developing,” not “failing.”

Quality (4 levels): Needs improvement · Developing · Proficient · Exemplary. Good for later courses, or when this collaboration score sits alongside a product rubric on the same scale.

Using AI to Draft the Wording

Step 3 — writing level descriptors in your own course language — is the slow part, and it's the part AI is genuinely good at. The prompts below assume you've already chosen your behaviors; that judgment is yours and shouldn't be outsourced. Copy a prompt, fill the brackets, and edit what comes back.

1 · Draft the level descriptors
I'm building a rubric row for a college course. The behavior I'm assessing is: [PASTE ONE BEHAVIOR]. The assignment is: [DESCRIBE THE TASK IN 2–3 SENTENCES, INCLUDING WHAT STUDENTS HAND IN]. My scale is: [Not yet / Approaching / Meets the goal]. Write one descriptor for each level. Each descriptor must describe something I could see in the work students submit — not their attitude, effort, or intent. Keep each under 25 words. The difference between adjacent levels should be a difference in what's visible, not a difference in adjectives.

That last sentence matters most. Without it you get “good / very good / excellent,” which is an adjective ladder, not a rubric.

2 · Translate a behavior into your discipline
Here is a general behavior from a collaboration framework: [PASTE ONE BEHAVIOR]. My course is [COURSE AND LEVEL — e.g., an introductory geology lab, a 200-level interpersonal communication course]. Rewrite it so it names something a student in my course would actually do, using the materials and activities of my discipline. Keep it observable. Give me three options.

Useful when a behavior reads as generic to your department. Pick the option that sounds like your course, not the most impressive one.

3 · Pressure-test a row you wrote
Here is a rubric row I wrote: [PASTE THE CRITERION AND ALL LEVELS]. Check it for three problems: (1) any level that relies on a judgment word like "actively," "appropriately," or "effectively" without saying what that looks like; (2) adjacent levels that differ only by degree words rather than by an observable difference; (3) anything a student couldn't tell in advance how to achieve. Quote the exact phrase for each problem you find and suggest a replacement. If a level is fine, say so.

The most valuable of the five. This is the same test used to write the behavior bank above.

4 · Check for double-counting
Here are the criteria in my rubric: [PASTE ALL CRITERIA]. Tell me whether any two overlap enough that a student could lose points twice for the same behavior. If so, say which pair, and recommend which one to cut or how to narrow them.

Easy to miss when you lift several behaviors from the same indicator.

5 · Write the student-facing version
Turn this rubric into a short "what I'm looking for" note for students, in plain second-person language: [PASTE THE RUBRIC]. Under 150 words. Don't restate the levels — tell students what to actually do. Assume some of them have had bad experiences with group work.

Students rarely read a rubric table. They read three sentences at the top of an assignment.

Four things to hold onto

  • The AI doesn't know your course. It will confidently invent specifics — assignment details, deadlines, discipline conventions. Delete anything you didn't tell it.
  • Don't paste student work. Prompt with your own assignment language and the behavior bank, never with something a student submitted.
  • The CPE milestone language is the anchor. If AI output drifts away from the milestone target, the milestone wins — that's the part you have to be able to defend.
  • You're drafting faster, not deciding less. Which behaviors, which weights, and what “meets the goal” means in your course are still your calls. Follow your institution's AI use policy.

Keep It Honest and Usable

  • Pick two to four criteria, not all sixteen behaviors — a focused rubric gets used; an exhaustive one gets ignored.
  • Choose behaviors you can actually observe in your course — in a discussion, a planning document, a peer review, or a reflection — not impressions you would have to guess at.
  • Check the level before you choose. 8.3 and 8.4 aren't available in a short activity, so lifting their behaviors into a Quick assignment leaves you with nothing to score.
  • The same behavior can be rated by the student, by peers, and by you; decide who is best positioned to see each one before you assign it.
  • Match the scale to the course level. Holding a first-year student to the milestone target and grading them down for being at benchmark penalizes them for being exactly where the framework expects.
