9.1 Quick Collaboration: Idea Bank

Short, low-stakes moves that build Indicator 8.1 — contributing ideas toward a shared goal. Sorted by field, meant to be borrowed.

Level 1 · Quick  ·  Builds 8.1  ·  Borrow Freely

This is a brainstorm, not a worked plan. Every idea here is Quick: one objective, one class session or a short online window, no standing teams, no group grade. Students contribute an idea into a shared space — that's already Skill 8 at the 8.1 level. What separates Quick from Structured is that there's no required back-and-forth: students post, they don't have to respond.

To run any of these online: the contribution lives in a Blackboard Discussion for asynchronous — in the chat or a breakout room of a Teams meeting for live. Give async a 24–48 hour window so working and caregiving students can take part on their own time.

Works in Any Course

  • Think-pair-share. Pose a question tied to the day's objective; students form an idea, trade with one partner, then one idea per pair goes to the whole group.
  • Idea wall. Everyone adds at least one idea toward a shared goal; the class then clusters them and names the themes together.
  • Pick and defend. A short scenario with two or three options — pick one and give a single reason tied to the goal, not just a preference.
  • One-minute paper, passed on. Write a short response, hand it to a neighbor, who adds one thing the first person missed.
  • Build the list. Each student adds one item nobody has posted yet. Late contributors have to read what's there first, which is the point.
  • Best question. Instead of an answer, each student contributes one question the group would need answered to reach the goal. Lower stakes than being right, and often more useful.

By Field

These are browsing aids, not boundaries. Steal from any cluster — a hand-off round works fine as a shift-change round, a spec check, or a peer-review checklist.

Health Sciences & Allied Health

  • Risk round. Each names one thing that could go wrong in a given procedure and one simple way to prevent it.
  • Care-goal contribution. Given a patient scenario, each posts one recommendation tied to the stated care goal.
  • Priority pick. Three possible actions — which would you do first, and why? The reasoning is the contribution.
  • Plain-language swap. Each offers one way to say a clinical term so a patient would actually understand it.

Technical & Trades

  • Pre-task hazard round. Each names one hazard for today's task and one control for it.
  • Method pick. Two valid approaches to the same job — pick one and give a reason tied to the outcome, not habit.
  • Troubleshoot the symptom. Given a described fault, each posts one likely cause nobody has named yet.
  • Done-check. Each names one thing they'd verify before calling the job finished.

Business, IT & Office

  • Budget pick. Limited money, three options — choose one move and give one reason it's the best use of it.
  • Customer problem round. Each contributes one possible cause of a described issue before anyone proposes a fix.
  • Requirements gathering. Each posts one question they'd ask a client before starting the work.
  • Cut one step. Given a process, each names one step to remove or change, with a reason.

General Education & First-Year

  • Evidence round. Each posts one piece of evidence relevant to the class question — and says what it shows.
  • In your own words. Each offers a plain-language definition of a key term; the class compares and builds the best version.
  • Where I'd start. Before solving, each posts their first step and why. Reasoning is visible even when answers aren't in yet.
  • Success habit. Each names one habit that helps a working student stay on track, and one reason it works.

Making One Yours

  • Tie it to the day's objective. Students can't contribute toward a goal they haven't been told. Name the goal before you open the floor.
  • Don't grade it. Quick folds into participation. A glance at the thread shows who contributed — that's all the record you need.
  • “One nobody has said yet” does real work online. It forces students to read the thread before posting, which is the async equivalent of listening.
  • If you start writing a rubric or assigning roles, you've moved up a level. That's fine — but check 4.0 first, because it costs more to run.
  • Adult and dual-credit learners: a 24–48 hour async window lets students contribute around work and caregiving, and Blackboard adds no new account or install.
